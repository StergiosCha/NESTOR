2. What is missing — the human annotation

The LLM judge scores reasoning at scale, but nobody has checked whether the judge is any good. That is what human annotation is for: we score the same explanations by hand, then measure whether the judge agrees with people. Without it, every claim about explanation quality in the paper rests on an unvalidated instrument.

So far: penny_kyriazi 419 item-reviews · dionisis_p 271 item-reviews. Only one model × dataset cell has been annotated by two people, so all agreement statistics currently rest on 200 items.

Why double annotation matters. A single person's scores tell us what that person thinks. Two people scoring the same item tell us whether the criteria mean anything consistent at all. If two trained annotators disagree, we cannot expect a model to agree with either — and that is a finding in itself. This is why every item needs two independent scores, and why annotators must not confer.

What you score

Each explanation gets three scores. Score all three for an item before moving on.

  • Phenomenon identification (0–2) — did the explanation name the right linguistic phenomenon (quantifier scope, monotonicity, plural reference, …)?
  • Soundness (0–2) — is the reasoning valid, independent of whether the final label was right?
  • Consistency (0–1) — does the explanation actually match the label the model gave?

One early result you should know about, because it makes your work matter. On the 200 items already double-annotated, the judge agrees with humans almost perfectly on consistency (κ = 0.94) and substantially on soundness (κ = 0.80) — but on phenomenon identification agreement is κ = −0.04, worse than chance, while two humans on the same items reach κ = 0.46.

The judge can tell whether an explanation is internally coherent, but it cannot tell whether the explanation identified the right phenomenon. That is a real limitation of automated evaluation — and it currently rests on 200 items. Your annotations are what turn it from a hint into a result.

How to run your annotation

  • You will be given 40 items, shared with one partner.
  • Do not discuss items with your partner while annotating. Independence is the point.
  • Use the notes field when you are unsure — disagreements are data, not mistakes.
  • Files are saved as reviews/<dataset>__<model>__<technique>__<lang>.<your_name>.reviews.json.

3. What happens with your work

  • Inter-annotator agreement (κ per criterion, across 10 pairs) — do the criteria mean something consistent?
  • Judge validation (κ judge vs human) — can automated evaluation replace human reading, and for which criteria?
  • Explanation quality by model and dataset, from both humans and judge.

All three go into the paper. The judge-reliability result in particular cannot be written without you.
